German *Stein* are famous the world over[^0], huge glass or ceramic mugs, filled to the top with beer in by women in traditional *Dirndl*, men stuffed into lederhosen, or mustachioed hipsters in metal band t-shirts. In Germany, most glasses are more modest, with only a 0.3 or 0.5 liter capacity, though a [*Maßkrug*](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma%C3%9Fkrug) can be a one or two liter monstrosity downed regularly at Oktoberfest. Germans are far from the only Europeans dedicated to the task of beer at volume. A half-liter cannette is my order of choice in Geneva[^1], while asking for a caneca gets you the same in Portugal. However, despite the popularity and existence of such drinking vessels, there's one thing they are never filled with at a bar or a restaurant: water.

<img src="https://nirvanb.github.io/nirvanb/images/post_img/Masskrug_neutral.jpg" alt="A Maßkrug, sourced from Wikimedia Commons" style="display:block; margin-left:auto; margin-right:auto" width="300">

To be sure, you can order water all over Europe, but most places you can pay(!) for a bottle of still or sparkling (*Sprüdel*) water, not a free and glorious carafe of tap water. The French and French-Swiss will accede to providing a *carafe d'eau*, though you are still likely to pay, and the Portuguese will bring you water by the 0.2 L glass. They expect you to parch your thirst a mouthful at a time![^2]

In Norway at least, an American can find succor. After a long day walking back around Oslo, I settled in for dinner at the excellent Le Benjamin, where in addition to an excellent Belgian wheat, a dry crisp white wine, and a decadent fortified desert wine to go with a superlative chocolate soufflee, I drank 4 carafes of water, constantly refilled! Oslo: where the water flows like ... water.

<img src="https://nirvanb.github.io/nirvanb/images/post_img/Souffle_LeBenjamin.jpeg" alt="An excellent chocolate souffle at Le Benjamine" style="display:block; margin-left:auto; margin-right:auto" width="300">

To be sure, some European cities have alternatives, like the gaping lions and other carved creatures which vomit potable water for public use throughout Geneva. Many countries also require bars and restaurants to provide you a glass of water free of charge if you request one, even just coming off the street. But as summer heatwaves become the norm, such public water availability will be more and more necessary. Water, especially clean drinking water, is not free, but I prefer that everyone have enough. In any case, it doesn't slow down my beer consumption. Prost!

[^0]: In German, *Stein* just means stone.
[^1]: Typically filled with a blonde beer, or a blanche (white wheat beer) with a slice of lemon.
[^2]: European service being what it is, getting a glass of water per hour would be quick stuff! That's why you need to order beer by the half liter.
